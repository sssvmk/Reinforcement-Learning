# David Silver's RL Lecture 1 — Introduction to Reinforcement Learning

**What RL is:** The "science of decision-making," sitting at the intersection of computer science, engineering (optimal control), neuroscience (dopamine systems), psychology (conditioning), economics (game theory), and math (operations research).

**What distinguishes RL** from supervised/unsupervised learning: no supervisor (only reward signals), delayed feedback, sequential non-IID data, and the agent's actions actively shape future data it sees.

**Reward hypothesis:** All goals can be expressed as maximizing expected cumulative scalar reward. Examples given: helicopter stunts, backgammon, portfolio management, power-station control, robot walking, and Atari game-playing (illustrated with videos).

**Core framework:** Agent–environment loop producing a history of observations/actions/rewards. Key distinctions in **state**: environment state (internal, often hidden), agent state (what the algorithm actually uses), and the Markov state (captures all useful history). Fully observable environments → Markov Decision Processes (MDPs); partially observable ones → POMDPs, requiring beliefs or learned state representations (e.g., RNNs).

**Agent components:** policy (state→action mapping), value function (expected future reward), and model (predicted dynamics/rewards) — giving a taxonomy of value-based, policy-based, and actor-critic agents, plus model-free vs. model-based RL.

**Key sub-problems:** learning vs. planning, exploration vs. exploitation (restaurant/advertising/oil-drilling analogies), and prediction vs. control.

**Course structure:** first half covers MDPs, dynamic programming, and model-free prediction/control; second half covers function approximation, policy gradients, and exploration at scale, ending with a games case study.

# David Silver's RL Lecture 2 — Markov Decision Processes

**Overview:** The lecture builds up formalism in stages — Markov Process → Markov Reward Process (MRP) → Markov Decision Process (MDP) — showing how almost any RL problem (including continuous-control, partially-observed, and bandit problems) can be cast as an MDP.

## Markov Process
A sequence of random states with the **Markov property**: the future is independent of the past given the present state. Defined by a state space `S` and transition probability matrix `P`. Illustrated with a "student Markov chain" toy example (class → Facebook → pub → sleep) where each row of `P` gives transition probabilities out of a state, and sampled episodes are random finite sequences through the chain.

## Markov Reward Process (MRP)
Adds a **reward function** `R` and **discount factor** `γ ∈ [0,1]` to a Markov process.
- **Return** `G_t`: discounted sum of future rewards for one sampled episode.
- **Discounting** is used for mathematical convenience (avoids infinite returns in cyclic processes), to reflect model uncertainty, to match financial time-value-of-money, and because it mirrors human/animal preference for immediate reward. Undiscounted formulations are possible when episodes are guaranteed to terminate, or via an average-reward formulation.
- **Value function** `V(s)`: expected return starting from state `s` (an expectation over random episodes, unlike the single-sample return).
- **Bellman equation** for MRPs: value = expected immediate reward + discounted value of the successor state. Illustrated with "backup diagrams" (one-step lookahead trees) and verified numerically on the student example.
- In matrix form, the Bellman equation is linear and can be solved directly via matrix inversion, but this is O(n³) and impractical for large state spaces — motivating iterative methods covered in later lectures (dynamic programming, Monte Carlo, temporal-difference learning).

## Markov Decision Process (MDP)
Adds a finite **action set** `A`; transitions and rewards now depend on the action taken, giving the agent real agency (the student chain becomes a decision chain with choices like study/Facebook/pub).
- **Policy** `π(a|s)`: a (generally stochastic) distribution over actions given state; stochastic policies support exploration. Because of the Markov property, optimal policies can always be stationary (time-independent).
- Fixing a policy collapses an MDP back into an MRP (by averaging transitions/rewards over the policy), so MRP machinery still applies.
- Two value functions: the **state-value function** `V_π(s)` (expected return from `s` under `π`) and the **action-value function** `Q_π(s,a)` (expected return from taking action `a` in `s`, then following `π`).
- **Bellman expectation equations** relate `V_π` and `Q_π` to each other and to themselves one step ahead, via lookahead diagrams that average over the policy's action choices and the environment's transition probabilities. Verified numerically on the student MDP.

## Optimality
- **Optimal value functions** `V*` and `Q*`: the best possible value achievable over all policies. Knowing `Q*` is sufficient to act optimally — just pick the action with the highest `Q*` in each state.
- There always exists at least one **deterministic optimal policy** `π*` for any MDP, and it is at least as good as every other policy in every state; all optimal policies share the same `V*`/`Q*`.
- **Bellman optimality equations**: `V*(s)` is the *max* (not average) over actions of the one-step lookahead; `Q*(s,a)` still *averages* over the environment's transition dynamics but uses `V*`/`Q*` of successor states. These equations are non-linear (due to the max), so no closed-form matrix solution exists — they must be solved iteratively, via methods like **value iteration**, **policy iteration** (dynamic programming, next lecture), and **Q-learning** (later lecture).

## Key Takeaways
- MDPs are the foundational formalism for RL: solving the Bellman optimality equation is equivalent to solving the MDP.
- Discounting, the agent/environment reward-indexing convention, and the distinction between evaluating a fixed policy (prediction) versus finding `π*` (control) are recurring themes carried into subsequent lectures on dynamic programming and model-free methods.

Rather than repeating that derivation, the notes below focus on what CS234 adds: motivation, framing, and course-specific concepts not covered in Silver's lecture.

**Why RL matters:** Framed as an automated agent learning *through experience* to make good decisions, complementary to "perceptual" ML (classification/recognition) and rooted in Bellman's work since the 1950s. Landmark successes cited: **AlphaGo** (RL + Monte Carlo tree search beating top humans at Go — a preview of Silver's Lecture 10 case studies), **fusion reactor plasma control** (deep RL for tokamak coil shaping), **COVID testing allocation in Greece** (RL-driven resource allocation at the border), and **ChatGPT** (behavior cloning/imitation learning → a learned reward model from human preference comparisons → RL fine-tuning, i.e., RLHF). **Reward hacking:** Illustrated via a "teaching agent" example (choosing addition/subtraction questions for a student) — a naively specified reward (e.g., minimizing response time) can incentivize degenerate behavior (always asking trivially easy questions) rather than genuine learning gains. **History vs. Markov state:** Reinforces Silver's agent/environment/Markov state distinctions from Lecture 1, noting the practical bias-variance tradeoff between compact Markov states (fast to learn with) and richer histories (more expressive but unbounded) — e.g., using the last 4 Atari frames rather than 1 to capture velocity/acceleration.

# David Silver's RL Lecture 3 — Planning by Dynamic Programming

**Overview:** Dynamic programming (DP) solves planning problems — where the MDP's dynamics and rewards are fully known — by breaking them into overlapping subproblems. MDPs satisfy the two properties DP requires: **optimal substructure** (the Bellman equation decomposes the problem recursively) and **overlapping subproblems** (the value function caches reusable solutions). The lecture covers prediction (policy evaluation) and control (finding the optimal policy) via policy iteration and value iteration.

## Policy Evaluation (Prediction)
Given an MDP and a fixed policy, repeatedly apply the **Bellman expectation equation** as a synchronous update across all states, using the previous iteration's values as the "leaves" of a one-step lookahead. Iterating converges to the true value function `V_π`. Demonstrated on a 4x4 grid-world example, where repeated backups converge to the expected number of steps under a random policy.

## Policy Iteration (Control)
Alternates two steps: **evaluate** the current policy (as above), then **improve** it by acting greedily with respect to the resulting value function. Repeating evaluate→improve→evaluate→improve is guaranteed to converge to the optimal policy and value function. Illustrated with Jack's Car Rental (a two-location vehicle-rental MDP), where only a few iterations were needed to reach the optimal policy. A formal argument shows each greedy improvement step is never worse than the previous policy, and improvement can only stop at a policy satisfying the Bellman optimality equation — i.e., at the optimum.

## Value Iteration (Control)
Instead of alternating full policy evaluation with improvement, iterate the **Bellman optimality equation** directly (one-step lookahead with a max over actions) across all states until convergence to `V*`. Equivalent to policy iteration with the evaluation step truncated to a single sweep (`k=1`). Intermediate value functions don't necessarily correspond to any real policy's value — they're just steps toward `V*`. Demonstrated via a shortest-path grid-world example showing values propagating backward from the goal.

## Complexity and Extensions
- State-value-based methods cost O(mn²) per sweep (m actions, n states); action-value (`Q`) versions cost more but avoid needing the transition model for action selection (useful later for model-free control).
- **Asynchronous DP** variants reduce computation by not sweeping every state every iteration (still converges if every state is visited infinitely often): **in-place DP** (always use the latest values), **prioritized sweeping** (update states with the largest Bellman error first), and **real-time DP** (update only states the agent actually visits).
- DP's "full-width backups" (considering every action and every successor state) don't scale to huge state spaces — motivating future lectures on **sampling-based backups** (Monte Carlo, temporal-difference learning), which don't require a known model.
- Convergence proofs for all these methods rely on the **contraction mapping** theorem (briefly mentioned, detailed in lecture notes).

## Companion Perspective: Stanford CS234 (Emma Brunskill), Lecture 2 — Tabular MDP Planning
CS234 covers the same policy/value iteration ground as above, with a few additions worth noting rather than repeating:

- **Analytic MRP solution:** Before introducing iterative DP, CS234 notes that a Markov Reward Process's value function can in principle be solved exactly in closed form, `V = (I − γP)⁻¹R`, via matrix inversion — exact but O(n³), which is precisely why the iterative/DP approach above is preferred for anything but small state spaces.
- **Formal contraction proof:** Where Silver's lecture only mentions the contraction-mapping theorem by name, CS234 works through the full proof that the Bellman optimality backup is a contraction under γ<1 — showing the L∞ distance between any two value-function vectors strictly shrinks (by at least a factor of γ) each time the backup is applied, which both guarantees convergence and proves the fixed point (optimal value function) is unique.
- **Formal monotonic-improvement proof for policy iteration:** A step-by-step derivation (via the Q-function and recursive substitution of the greedy action at each future time step) shows policy iteration's new policy is never worse than the old one, and strictly better unless already optimal — formalizing the guarantee Silver's lecture states more informally.
- **Finite-horizon value iteration:** Value iteration can be read as computing a *sequence* of optimal value functions/policies, one per remaining-horizon length `k = 1...H` — useful when the task has a fixed, finite number of decision steps rather than running indefinitely; optimal policies in this setting need not be stationary (time-independent).
- **Monte Carlo rollout evaluation:** As a practical alternative to exact Bellman backups when the dynamics model is hard to write down explicitly (e.g., very large or partially observed state spaces), a policy can be evaluated by simulating many trajectories and averaging the observed returns — accuracy improves as `1/√n` (via Hoeffding/Bernstein-style concentration bounds), and this approach doesn't require the Markov assumption to hold.

# David Silver's RL Lecture 4 — Model-Free Prediction

**Overview:** Having solved MDPs via dynamic programming when the model is known, the course turns to *model-free prediction*: estimating `V_π` purely from sampled experience, with no knowledge of the environment's transition or reward structure. Two families — Monte Carlo and Temporal-Difference (TD) learning — are introduced and then unified via TD(λ).

## Monte Carlo (MC) Learning
Learns only from **complete episodes**, estimating the value of a state as the empirical mean of sample returns observed from it. **First-visit MC** averages only the return following the first visit to a state per episode; **every-visit MC** averages every visit. Both converge to `V_π` by the law of large numbers, independent of state-space size. Illustrated on a simplified Blackjack MDP, where running thousands of episodes with a naive "stick only on 20/21" policy reveals the value surface purely from sampled outcomes, with no knowledge of the deck or dealer rules. Means can be updated incrementally (running average, or with a fixed step size for non-stationary problems) instead of batch-computed.

## Temporal-Difference (TD) Learning
Learns from **incomplete episodes** via **bootstrapping**: instead of waiting for the actual return, TD(0) updates toward the *TD target* — the immediate reward plus the discounted current estimate of the next state's value — generating a *TD error*. Illustrated with a commute-time example showing TD updates its prediction after every step rather than only at the end.

**MC vs. TD comparison:**
- TD can learn online, from incomplete sequences, and in continuing (non-episodic) tasks; MC requires terminating episodes.
- MC is unbiased but high variance (depends on the whole stochastic trajectory); TD is biased (bootstraps from a possibly-wrong estimate) but lower variance (only one step of randomness). TD typically converges faster in practice and provably converges to `V_π` under table lookup.
- With finite/repeated data, MC converges to the values minimizing squared error against observed returns, while TD converges to the solution of the maximum-likelihood MDP implied by the data — TD exploits the Markov property, which helps in Markov environments but can hurt under partial observability, where MC's lack of reliance on the Markov property is an advantage.

## Unifying View
DP, TD, and MC can be placed on two axes: whether they **bootstrap** (DP and TD do; MC doesn't) and whether they **sample** (TD and MC do; DP does full-width backups). **n-step returns** generalize TD(0) (n=1) and MC (n=∞) by bootstrapping after n real steps; an intermediate n often performs best but the optimal choice is problem-dependent.

## TD(λ)
Averages all n-step returns with geometrically decaying weights `(1−λ)λ^(n−1)` to form the **λ-return**, giving a tunable spectrum between TD(0) (λ=0) and MC (λ=1) that is more robust than picking a single fixed n (**forward view**). An equivalent, computationally efficient **backward view** uses **eligibility traces** — per-state decaying tags that combine frequency and recency of visitation — updating every state's value online in proportion to its current trace and the TD error, without waiting for episode end. The two views are provably equivalent for offline updates.

## Companion Perspective: Stanford CS234 (Emma Brunskill), Lecture 3 — Policy Evaluation
CS234 covers the same MC/TD(0) ground as above (including the same core MC-vs-TD divergence-on-finite-data insight), with a few additions worth noting rather than repeating:

- **Formal bias/variance/consistency framework:** Estimators are compared along three axes — **bias** (expected deviation from the true value), **variance**, and **consistency** (does the estimate converge to the true value as data → ∞). First-visit MC is unbiased and consistent (its per-state samples are i.i.d.); every-visit MC is biased (returns within one trajectory are correlated, not i.i.d.) but still consistent, often with better MSE since it reuses more data per trajectory. TD(0) is biased (bootstraps from a possibly-wrong estimate) but consistent under the same step-size conditions (`Σα_t = ∞`, `Σα_t² < ∞`) used for incremental MC.
- **Certainty-equivalence (model-based) evaluation:** A distinct third option beyond pure MC/TD — fit a maximum-likelihood MDP from the collected data (tabular counts of transitions/rewards), then run ordinary dynamic-programming policy evaluation on that fitted model. Far more data-efficient (a single observed transition updates the model for *every* state/action pair consistent with it, not just the states visited in that trajectory) but computationally heavier, since it solves a full MDP rather than updating single states.
- **Worked numerical example of MC/TD divergence:** A concrete two-state illustration (states A→B, 8 recorded episodes, γ=1) makes tangible the general claim above: replaying this fixed batch, TD(0) converges to `V(A)=0.75` — the value implied by the fitted MDP's Markov structure (reward in A plus the already-learned `V(B)=0.75`) — while Monte Carlo converges to `V(A)=0`, since the single episode that ever visited A happened to return 0, and MC simply minimizes squared error against observed returns with no notion that A's value should relate to B's.

# David Silver's RL Lecture 5 — Model-Free Control

**Overview:** Combines last lecture's model-free prediction tools with generalized policy iteration to find the *optimal* policy without knowing the environment's dynamics. Distinguishes **on-policy** learning (evaluating/improving the policy you're actually following) from **off-policy** learning (learning about a target policy while following a different behavior policy).

## On-Policy Monte Carlo Control
Plugging Monte Carlo evaluation into generalized policy iteration requires using **action-value functions Q** (not V), since greedy improvement from Q needs no model (just `argmax` over actions), whereas V-based improvement would require a model to look ahead. Pure greedy improvement risks never exploring some actions and getting stuck on incorrect estimates (illustrated with a two-door bandit example). **ε-greedy exploration** (random action with probability ε, greedy otherwise) fixes this and is provably a genuine policy-improvement step. For full convergence to the optimal policy, exploration must satisfy **GLEE** (Greedy in the Limit with Infinite Exploration): every state-action pair is visited infinitely often, and the policy becomes greedy in the limit — achieved by decaying ε (e.g., `1/k`). Updating Q and the policy after *every episode* (rather than waiting for full evaluation) is far more efficient, giving **GLIE Monte Carlo control**, demonstrated on Blackjack to recover the game's known optimal strategy.

## On-Policy TD Control: SARSA
Replacing Monte Carlo evaluation with TD lets updates happen after every single step rather than every episode. **SARSA** (State-Action-Reward-State-Action) updates `Q(s,a)` toward the TD target `r + γQ(s′,a′)`, where `a′` is the action actually selected by the current (e.g., ε-greedy) policy — making it on-policy. Converges to the optimal `Q*` under GLIE plus suitable step-size (Robbins-Monro) conditions, demonstrated on a "windy gridworld" example showing rapid learning-curve improvement once the goal is first found.

## SARSA(λ)
Generalizes SARSA using n-step Q-returns and a geometrically-weighted **λ-return**, interpolating between SARSA(λ=0) and Monte Carlo (λ=1). The efficient **backward view** uses **eligibility traces** over state-action pairs, propagating a reward signal backward through an entire trajectory in a single episode (rather than one step at a time), overcoming the slow backward propagation of plain SARSA.

## Off-Policy Learning
Motivated by: learning from observing others (e.g., humans), reusing old experience from earlier policies, learning a deterministic optimal policy while behaving exploratorily, and learning about multiple policies from one stream of experience. **Importance sampling** corrects for the mismatch between behavior and target policy distributions, but is very high-variance over full Monte Carlo trajectories (often impractical) and only moderately better for one-step TD.

**Q-learning** avoids importance sampling entirely: it samples the next action from the behavior policy but bootstraps using a value computed under the target policy. In the well-known special case where the target policy is greedy (and behavior is ε-greedy), this reduces to updating `Q(s,a)` toward `r + γ·max_a′ Q(s′,a′)` — directly sampling the Bellman optimality equation, and provably converges to `Q*` while allowing both policies to keep improving.

## Unifying Summary
DP methods (policy evaluation, policy iteration, value iteration) correspond respectively to the *sampled* versions TD learning, SARSA, and Q-learning — in each case, replacing the full expectation in a Bellman equation with a single sampled transition.

## Companion Perspective: Stanford CS234 (Emma Brunskill), Lecture 4 — Q-Learning and Function Approximation
Adds an important conceptual subtlety behind GLIE Monte Carlo control's convergence: the running `Q` estimate is *not* an estimate of `Q` for any single policy `π_k` — it's a strange weighted average blended across every past (constantly-changing, constantly-more-deterministic) policy, built from just one noisy rollout per episode. That this nonetheless converges to `Q*` is a genuinely non-obvious, almost surprising result, resting on **Robbins-Monro step-size conditions** (`Σ_t α_t = ∞` and `Σ_t α_t² < ∞`, satisfied e.g. by `α=1/t`) layered on top of GLIE, and ultimately justified by 1990s stochastic-approximation theory for both SARSA and Q-learning in the tabular setting.

# David Silver's RL Lecture 6 — Value Function Approximation

**Overview:** Addresses scaling RL to large/continuous state spaces (backgammon ~10^20 states, Go ~10^170, continuous control) where tabular methods are infeasible. Solution: represent V(s) or Q(s,a) with a parameterized function approximator (weights w) that generalizes across states, trained via supervised-learning-style methods but adapted for RL's non-stationary, non-IID, correlated data.

## Architectures
Three Q-function designs: (1) state-value "black box," (2) action-in (state+action → single value), (3) action-out (state → all action values in one pass, used in DQN/Atari). Focus is on differentiable approximators: linear combinations of features (simple, convex, globally optimizable) and neural networks. Table lookup is shown to be a special case of linear approximation using one-hot indicator features per state.

## Incremental Prediction
Uses stochastic gradient descent to minimize mean-squared error against a target. Since no "oracle" exists, targets are substituted: Monte Carlo returns (unbiased but noisy), TD(0) targets (biased due to bootstrapping through the approximator itself), or TD(λ) returns (forward view, or backward view via eligibility traces sized to the weight vector). Linear TD is proven (Tsitsiklis & Van Roy) to converge near the global optimum despite bias.

## Incremental Control
Same ideas applied to Q via generalized policy iteration — approximate policy evaluation plus ε-greedy improvement, with Monte Carlo/TD/TD(λ)/SARSA targets. Illustrated via the Mountain Car task (continuous position/velocity state, linear/tile-coded features), showing SARSA(λ) carving out the value-function surface. Empirically, intermediate λ (bootstrapping) usually outperforms both pure TD(0) and pure Monte Carlo.

## Convergence Issues
TD methods aren't always stable — off-policy or nonlinear bootstrapping can diverge; control methods generally only guarantee "chattering" near a good policy, not full convergence. Gradient TD / emphatic TD methods were introduced to fix this via true gradient descent on the projected Bellman error.

## Batch Methods and DQN
Least-squares methods fit the approximator to *all* stored experience at once rather than discarding data after one update. Experience replay implements this practically: store transitions, sample random mini-batches, and repeatedly apply SGD — this decorrelates sequential data. DQN (the Atari breakthrough) combines Q-learning + experience replay + a **frozen target network** (updated only periodically) to stabilize training with neural networks — achieving human-level play across 50 games. For linear approximation specifically, least-squares TD (LSTD/LSTD(λ)) solves for w in closed form via matrix inversion, converging on- and off-policy; LSPI (Least-Squares Policy Iteration) extends this to control, demonstrated on a 50-state random-walk chain converging to the optimal policy within ~7 iterations.

## Companion Perspective: Stanford CS234 (Emma Brunskill), Lecture 4 — Q-Learning and Function Approximation
Gives a sharper, more mechanistic account of *why* the combination of bootstrapping + function approximation + off-policy learning (the **"Deadly Triad,"** per Sutton & Barto) can cause divergence rather than mere instability: the tabular Bellman backup is a provable **contraction** (shrinks the distance between any two value estimates), but fitting a function approximator to that backup's outputs can act as an **expansion** (it can *increase* the distance between estimates) — a result visualized concretely in a well-known 1995 example by Geoffrey Gordon. Alternating a contraction with an expansion offers no guarantee of converging to a fixed point at all, which is why naive Q-learning with linear or neural-network function approximation was historically viewed with real suspicion through the 1990s.

DQN's two fixes map onto this diagnosis: **experience replay** breaks the correlated, non-IID sequence of updates and dramatically improves data reuse/sample efficiency, while **fixed target networks** stop the bootstrap target from shifting on every gradient step (making training resemble ordinary supervised regression with a stable label). Published ablations show both mattered, but **experience replay alone drove by far the largest gains** — boosting scores from roughly 3–10 up to 241 in some games (orders of magnitude), with fixed targets contributing a further, smaller but still meaningful improvement on top; a plain, more powerful (deep) function approximator *without* these two tricks was not reliably better than a simple linear one. DQN's broader significance: a single CNN architecture and hyperparameter set, trained separately per game purely from raw pixels, achieved strong play across dozens of distinct Atari titles.

# David Silver's RL Lecture 7 — Policy Gradient Methods

**Overview:** Shifts from estimating value functions to directly parameterizing the policy itself (parameters θ) and improving it via gradient ascent on expected reward, rather than deriving actions implicitly from Q.

## Why Policy-Based Methods
Better convergence properties (smooth incremental updates vs. the chattering/divergence possible with value-based max operators); effective in continuous or very high-dimensional action spaces where computing `argmax` over actions is expensive or impossible; and can represent genuinely **stochastic optimal policies** — necessary in adversarial games with no safe deterministic strategy (e.g., rock-paper-scissors) and under **state aliasing/partial observability**, where indistinguishable states require randomization to avoid infinite oscillation (illustrated with a grid-world example). Downsides: naive policy gradient can be slower and higher-variance than value-based methods.

## Objective Functions
Three essentially equivalent formulations — expected return from a start state, average value over the stationary distribution, or average reward per time step — all yield the same gradient direction.

## Finite-Difference Policy Gradient
A naive numerical approach perturbing each parameter dimension to estimate the gradient; works even for non-differentiable policies but scales poorly with dimensionality (demonstrated via a robot-gait-tuning example).

## Analytic (Monte Carlo) Policy Gradient
Uses the **likelihood-ratio/score-function trick** (∇log π) to rewrite the gradient as an expectation that can be sampled from experience. Score functions are derived for the **softmax policy** (discrete actions) and **Gaussian policy** (continuous actions). The **policy gradient theorem** generalizes the one-step bandit case to full MDPs by replacing immediate reward with the action-value function Q. The resulting **REINFORCE** algorithm updates parameters using the sampled return, but suffers from high variance and slow learning.

## Actor-Critic Methods
Replace the noisy Monte Carlo return with a learned, approximate Q (the "critic"), while the "actor" updates the policy itself — combining the two prior lectures' toolkits. Variance-reduction techniques include: a zero-mean **baseline** (any function of state, not action, leaves the gradient unbiased), with the **state value function V** as the natural choice, yielding the **advantage function** A = Q − V; using the **TD error** as a cheap, unbiased sample of the advantage (needs only a V-critic, not Q); and **eligibility traces** to blend information across multiple time steps (directly analogous to TD(λ)). **Compatible function approximation** shows that choosing critic features equal to the score function guarantees the approximate gradient is unbiased. The lecture closes with the **deterministic policy gradient** (avoids the variance blow-up of shrinking stochastic policies, uses ∇Q directly — effective for continuous control) and a unifying summary table of all actor-critic variants (differing in critic target and actor update rule).

## Companion Perspective: Stanford CS234 (Emma Brunskill), Lectures 5–6 — Policy Search 1 & 2
Adds several derivation-level and algorithmic points not spelled out above. First, expanding a trajectory's probability as a product of the start-state distribution, the policy's per-step action probabilities, and the environment's transition probabilities shows explicitly that **the transition-dynamics term is independent of θ and vanishes under the gradient** — so the likelihood-ratio policy gradient needs no dynamics model, and this holds even if the *environment* is non-Markov (only the *policy* needs to be well-defined, whether Markov-in-state or history-dependent). Second, a **temporal-causality** argument justifies replacing "reward of the whole trajectory" with "return-to-go" in the REINFORCE-style update on cleaner grounds: since an action at time t cannot influence rewards received before t, weighting each step's score function only by its own return-to-go is a strictly lower-variance, still-unbiased rewrite of the raw full-trajectory estimator — distinct from, and more primitive than, baseline subtraction. Third, a step-by-step proof confirms the state-only baseline's unbiasedness (the baseline term factors out of a future-trajectory expectation and reduces to the gradient of a constant, since action probabilities sum to one).

Beyond the shared baseline/actor-critic material, this companion lecture raises two practical limitations of vanilla policy gradient not addressed above: (1) updates are strictly **on-policy** — a gradient estimated from one policy's rollouts cannot be reused after even a single update, unlike Q-learning's off-policy reuse; (2) a fixed step size in **parameter space** can produce wildly disproportionate changes in **policy/decision space** (e.g., a small shift in a softmax's θ can flip an action's probability from 50% to near-100%), risking a performance collapse onto a flat plateau with no guarantee of monotonic improvement.

This motivates the **performance difference lemma**: a new policy's value equals the old policy's value plus the old policy's advantage function, expectated over the *new* policy's state-action distribution — still unusable directly (it needs new-policy rollouts), but reweighting actions via an **importance-sampling ratio** `π′(a|s)/π(a|s)` and approximating the new policy's state distribution with the old one's yields a fully old-data-computable surrogate objective, whose approximation error is formally bounded by the **KL divergence** between the two policies. This underlies **TRPO** (maximize the surrogate subject to/penalized by a KL constraint, enabling multiple reused-data gradient steps per rollout) and its simpler, now-dominant descendant **PPO**, which instead **clips** the importance ratio to `[1−ε, 1+ε]` to prevent exploding/vanishing advantage weighting when the policies diverge — achieving similar trust-region safety with far less complexity and substantially better empirical sample efficiency, which is why PPO became the standard choice (including in RLHF pipelines like ChatGPT's).

# David Silver's RL Lecture 8 — Integrating Learning and Planning

**Overview:** Shifts focus to **model-based RL**: explicitly learning a model of the environment's dynamics/rewards from experience, then using that model to *plan* (look ahead) — rather than learning a value function or policy directly from real experience.

## Model-Based RL Basics
A model is a learned approximation of an MDP's transition dynamics and reward function (treating state/action spaces as known). It's learned via supervised learning: state-action pairs → next state (density estimation) and → reward (regression), with table-lookup models simply counting transitions (illustrated via the earlier "AB" example). **Advantages**: a model can be a far more compact/easier learning target than the value function itself (e.g., in chess the rules are trivial to learn, while the value function is extremely complex and sharp); model learning doesn't require waiting for full trajectories, and models can support reasoning about uncertainty for exploration. **Disadvantage**: two sources of approximation error (model + value function) compound, and performance is capped by the quality of the learned model.

## Planning and Sample-Based Planning
Once a model exists, planning solves the resulting MDP (via DP, tree search, etc.). **Sample-based planning** treats the learned model as a simulator: sample trajectories from it and apply ordinary model-free RL (e.g., Monte Carlo) to the simulated experience — this is efficient because sampling focuses effort on likely outcomes rather than exhaustively enumerating all transitions.

## Dyna Architecture
Combines both worlds: learn a value function/policy from **both** real experience (direct RL) and simulated experience generated by a learned model (planning). The canonical **Dyna-Q** algorithm updates Q from each real step (and updates the model), then performs extra "imagined" Q-learning updates by replaying sampled past state-actions through the model. Demonstrated on grid-world mazes: increasing planning steps per real step dramatically improves data efficiency; **Dyna-Q+** adds an exploration bonus for long-unvisited states, helping the agent adapt when the environment changes.

## Simulation-Based Search (Forward Search)
Rather than solving the whole MDP, focus only on the sub-MDP reachable from the *current* state by building a search tree rooted at "now," sampling trajectories from the model, and applying RL to them. **Simple Monte Carlo search** evaluates each root action by averaging returns over many simulated rollouts. **Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS)** improves on this by recording Q-values at every visited node (not just the root), improving the simulation policy during search (exploiting promising lines while still exploring), and provably converging to the optimal search tree — illustrated via the ancient board game Go, where MCTS drove dramatic jumps in computer-Go strength (well past earlier heuristic programs). **TD search** generalizes the idea by replacing Monte Carlo backups with TD/SARSA-style bootstrapping within the search tree, which helps especially when states are reachable via multiple paths. **Dyna-2** extends this further, combining a long-term memory value function (learned from real experience, general domain knowledge) with a short-term memory (search tree, learned from simulated experience specific to the current situation) — shown to outperform either approach alone in Go.

# David Silver's RL Lecture 9 — Exploration and Exploitation

**Overview:** Tackles the fundamental trade-off in online RL between *exploiting* current knowledge (acting greedily) and *exploring* to gather information that could improve future decisions. Mostly examined via the **multi-armed bandit** (one-state, one-step MDP), with extensions to contextual bandits and full MDPs. Three broad strategies covered: random exploration, optimism in the face of uncertainty, and information-state-space (theoretically optimal) methods. Also distinguishes **state-action exploration** (systematic, remembers what's been tried) from **parameter exploration** (perturbing policy parameters directly — common in robotics, consistent but ignorant of the state-action space).

## Regret
Performance is measured via **total regret**: cumulative shortfall versus always picking the best arm, expressible as Σ(count of each suboptimal action × its "gap" to optimal). Naive approaches (greedy, fixed-ε-greedy, optimistic initialization without ongoing exploration) all suffer **linear regret** — they can get stuck or keep making the same mistake indefinitely. A key goal is achieving **sublinear (ideally logarithmic) regret**, and there's a proven theoretical lower bound (logarithmic in time, scaled by gap size and distribution similarity/KL divergence) that no algorithm can beat — interestingly, a well-tuned *decaying* ε-greedy schedule (if gaps were known) can actually hit this bound, though in practice the schedule is unknown in advance.

## Optimism in the Face of Uncertainty
Core principle: prefer actions with the most *potential* upside, not just the current best estimate — trying an uncertain arm narrows its distribution over time. Implemented as **Upper Confidence Bound (UCB)** methods: track a value estimate plus a shrinking "bonus" term reflecting confidence-interval width (derived via Hoeffding's inequality, distribution-free), always picking the action with highest combined score (UCB1 algorithm achieves logarithmic regret and performs strongly in practice, often outclassing naive ε-greedy). A **Bayesian** variant uses an assumed prior (e.g., Gaussian) over Q-values, updating posteriors and using mean + k·stddev as the bonus — more powerful if the prior is accurate. A closely related idea, **probability matching**, selects actions proportional to the belief they're optimal; its simple sampling-based implementation, **Thompson sampling** (sample from each arm's posterior, pick the best sample), is old (1930s) yet provably asymptotically optimal.

## Information State Space
The theoretically "correct" (but computationally expensive) approach: treat the agent's accumulated knowledge (e.g., per-arm success/failure counts) as part of an augmented MDP state. Transitions move between information states as actions are taken; solving this (now much larger) MDP yields the exact optimal exploration-exploitation trade-off given a prior. For Bernoulli bandits this becomes **Bayes-adaptive RL** with Beta-distributed posteriors, solvable in principle via dynamic programming (**Gittins indices**), or more tractably via Monte Carlo Tree Search over the information-state MDP.

## Extensions
**Contextual bandits** reintroduce state (context) — e.g., selecting banner ads/news articles based on user features — solvable via linear UCB-style methods (real-world deployments like Yahoo's front-page news optimization). Extending to full **MDPs**: UCB-style bonuses can be added to Q-values (though this is imperfect since Q-value uncertainty conflates both estimation error and future-policy improvement); a popular heuristic is **optimistic model-based RL** (e.g., RMAX) — treat unvisited states as maximally rewarding ("heaven") so planning naturally drives exploration toward them until their true (lower) value is learned; and the information-state-space idea also generalizes to MDPs by augmenting the real state with accumulated information, solvable approximately via MCTS, though not yet scaled to very complex domains.

## Companion Perspective: Stanford CS234 (Emma Brunskill), Lectures 11–13 — Exploration 1, 2 & 3
Motivates the whole data-efficient-exploration unit by contrasting simulator-rich domains (Atari, Go, where compute and data are interchangeable) with real-world domains (healthcare, education, marketing, conservation) where samples are genuinely scarce and expensive — making deliberate exploration strategy, not just asymptotic guarantees, practically important. Proposes three axes for evaluating an RL algorithm: does it converge at all (not guaranteed, per the Deadly Triad), does it converge to the *optimal* policy, and *how fast*; also distinguishes performance profiles that are "always decent" versus "usually great but occasionally terrible" (illustrated via an AI-clinician framing — 80% average benefit for everyone vs. complete success for 80% and failure for 20%).

Complements the optimism-under-uncertainty principle with its mirror-image failure mode: a **pessimistic** action-selection rule (always pick the arm with the highest *lower* confidence bound) can produce **linear regret**, because once an arm's lower bound dips below a rival's, it is never resampled and its estimate never updates — unlike optimism, where every pull either yields high reward or shrinks uncertainty, pessimism has no such self-correcting property. This underscores *why* optimism specifically (not just "track confidence intervals") is the right exploration principle.

A real deployed case study (Greece's COVID border-testing bandit, "Eva") illustrates how far bandit theory must stretch in practice: a **non-stationary, contextual, batch** bandit with **delayed feedback** (outcomes arrive a day or two after a whole planeload of decisions must be made) and resource/political **constraints** that push toward population-level rather than purely per-individual policies. This delayed/batched-feedback setting exposes a key practical weakness of UCB-style algorithms: they freeze on their current best guess until confidence bounds can be updated, whereas **Thompson sampling naturally keeps exploring** every round by resampling from its existing posteriors even without new data — shown empirically (Yahoo ad/news recommendation) to degrade far less than UCB as feedback delay grows. The flip side: Thompson sampling can perform badly if its prior is strongly misleading, since data can take a long time to overwhelm a sharp wrong prior, whereas optimism algorithms carry no such prior-dependence (though they generally have tighter worst-case regret bounds that don't always translate to better empirical performance).

Introduces the **PAC (Probably Approximately Correct)** framework as an alternative to regret for evaluating exploration algorithms: with high probability, an algorithm is PAC if it picks an ε-optimal action on all but a polynomial number of time steps — unlike regret, PAC only penalizes genuinely bad (more than ε below optimal) choices, not merely-suboptimal ones close to the best arm. Also notes a practical pitfall with naive **optimistic initialization** (as an alternative to explicit UCB bonuses): the "high enough" initial value is domain-dependent and unknown in advance, can still lock onto a suboptimal arm if set wrong, and is especially hard to apply under function approximation (no clear way to initialize a neural network so every reachable state gets an optimistic value). Finally, frames the question of true optimality: computing the exact-optimal Bayesian bandit policy is in principle a (PO)MDP-planning problem over belief states, but is generally intractable; **index policies** (greedy and UCB both qualify) sidestep this by scoring each arm using only its own statistics, and **Gittins indices** are a provably optimal index policy for discounted Bayesian bandits — notably, Thompson sampling is not equivalent to the Gittins index and is not optimal in general, despite strong empirical performance.

Lecture 13 completes the unit by rigorously lifting both exploration principles from bandits to full MDPs. On the optimism side, it fills in the mechanics behind the RMAX-style heuristic already noted above: an **MBIE-style algorithm** maintains visitation counts and empirical reward/dynamics models, but instead of simply treating unvisited states as "heaven," it runs repeated Bellman backups with an explicit, shrinking count-based bonus term added to each backup — provably propagating optimism backward through the MDP and yielding a genuinely **PAC** algorithm (not just a heuristic). A **simulation lemma** underpins the PAC proof: it bounds the worst-case value-function gap between two MDPs (for a fixed policy) by their reward-model error plus their dynamics-model error (scaled by max value and discount), formalizing that shrinking model error guarantees improving value accuracy.

On the Bayesian side, it extends Thompson sampling to MDPs via **PSRL (Posterior Sampling for RL)**: maintain priors over the reward and dynamics models (a Dirichlet prior per state-action pair for transitions, conjugate to the multinomial), and at the *start of each episode* — not after every step — sample one full MDP, solve it exactly, and commit to the resulting policy for the whole episode. This per-episode commitment (rather than continuous resampling) is deliberate: resampling mid-episode can cause **policy thrashing** (demonstrated via a chain-MDP example where the agent repeatedly reverses direction as its belief about which end is better flips back and forth). For multi-agent/concurrent exploration of the same environment, **seed sampling** coordinates agents toward different hypotheses to avoid redundant exploration — though theory suggests such coordination isn't strictly necessary for a near-linear speedup with more agents, even though it helps empirically.

Finally, addresses generalization beyond the tabular case: **contextual bandits** with a **linear reward model** (reward as a linear function of shared state-action features) let information generalize across a huge number of "arms," controlling regret growth via an uncertainty set over the parameter vector (e.g., LinUCB, deployed for real-world news recommendation) — notably, even a linear reward model can produce a piecewise-linear (disjoint-linear) optimal policy. For MDPs with huge/continuous state spaces (e.g., Atari), literal visitation counts are meaningless, so **pseudo-counts** (density-model-based generalized counts) substitute for the count-based bonus — shown to be critical for progress on notoriously hard sparse-reward games like *Montezuma's Revenge*, where plain ε-greedy DQN made no progress at all. Analogous generalizations of Thompson sampling include bootstrapped Q-ensembles and Bayesian linear regression on a network's last layer. A final frontier is **meta-exploration across many tasks**: **Decision Pre-trained Transformers (DPT)** are trained via supervised learning to predict the *optimal* action (not just imitate past behavior), which provably recovers Thompson-sampling-equivalent behavior and its guarantees, while empirically learning to exploit latent task structure (e.g., shared linear reward structure) without being told it exists.

# David Silver's RL Lecture 10 — Classic Games

**Overview:** A capstone lecture showing how all the course's RL and planning tools combine to achieve superhuman performance in classic board/card games — framed as an "IQ test" for AI, with simple rules but deep strategy.

## Game Theory Basics
Optimality in multi-agent settings is captured by the **Nash equilibrium**: a joint policy where every player's strategy is a best response to everyone else's — unlike single-agent "best response," which just treats opponents as part of the environment. For two-player zero-sum perfect-information games, a unique Nash equilibrium exists and corresponds exactly to the **minimax value function** (the outcome under optimal play by both sides). Crucially, self-play RL — each agent learning best responses to its own evolving opponent — provably converges to a Nash equilibrium for a wide class of standard RL algorithms (Littman's result).

## Minimax Search
The classical planning approach: build a search tree, propagate terminal outcomes back via alternating max/min, and (since exhaustive search is infeasible) truncate at shallow depth using a learned **value function** at the leaves (as in deep search engines with Alpha-Beta pruning). Case studies: **Deep Blue** (chess) used ~8,000 hand-crafted features and massively parallel search with no learning; **Chinook** (checkers) combined hand-tuned features, Alpha-Beta search, and retrograde endgame databases, eventually leading to checkers being *perfectly solved*.

## Self-Play Reinforcement Learning
Standard TD/Monte Carlo methods apply almost unchanged to self-play: update value estimates toward the outcome of the game (win/loss), using "after-states" for efficient deterministic move evaluation, with one player maximizing and the other minimizing. Case studies: **Logistello** (Othello) discovered ~1.5M board-pattern features via self-play and reached superhuman play; **TD-Gammon** (backgammon) used a neural network trained purely by self-play TD learning — stable here (unlike chess/checkers) because dice-roll stochasticity smooths the value function; later retraining with more compute and zero hand-crafted knowledge reached superhuman level.

## Combining Search and Learning
Pure TD struggled in highly tactical games lacking stochastic smoothing, motivating hybrids: **TD-root** (Samuel's original 1959 checkers program) updates the root value toward a deep search's result; **TD-leaf** updates toward the next step's searched value along the principal variation (used in chess program KnightCap and in Chinook, reaching Master/superhuman levels); **TreeStrap** goes further, using *every* node's search value as a training target within a single search tree, proving more robust from random initialization (strong chess results against International Masters). Convergence is empirical/partial in these richer settings and requires "grounding" with real game outcomes.

## Simulation-Based Search Revisited
Monte Carlo Tree Search with UCB-style exploration at each node (**UCT**) extends self-play to simulation-based search, proven to converge to minimax-optimal values, and has outperformed Alpha-Beta search in games with large branching factors or no good value function (Go, Hex, and later even backgammon). **Maven** (Scrabble) combined a learned rack-value function with simple Monte Carlo rollouts, reaching near-perfect play (famous "fishing" endgame win over world champion Adam Logan) despite ignoring hidden opponent-tile information.

## Imperfect Information Games
Naively applying self-play MCTS to hidden-information games (e.g., poker) diverges. **Smooth UCT** fixes this by blending standard UCT with play that's robust against the *average* of all previously observed opponent strategies (inspired by fictitious play), letting each player maintain and share simulation statistics across separate per-player search trees — reaching competitive, near-Nash poker performance and even outperforming a near-perfect Nash solver in practice due to better exploitation of weaker opponents.

## Closing Recipe
Across all these successes: binary/handcrafted input features → a (usually linear, later neural) value function → trained via some member of the TD family → via self-play → combined with search (Alpha-Beta or Monte Carlo variants). The lecture closes noting the search-vs-knowledge trade-off, and that deep neural networks were already beginning to challenge the assumption that search is always necessary (foreshadowing later Go breakthroughs).

# Companion Perspective: Stanford CS234 (Emma Brunskill), Lectures 8–10 & DPO Guest Lecture — Imitation Learning, Inverse RL, RLHF, DPO & Offline RL

**Overview:** Content not covered in David Silver's original course — included here as its own section since it extends beyond the Silver syllabus into imitation learning, inverse reinforcement learning, and learning from human feedback (the technique underlying ChatGPT).

## Recap: Imitation Learning
Reviews **behavior cloning** (reducing RL to supervised learning by directly mapping states to expert actions) and **DAGGER** (keeps an expert in the loop to correct distribution-mismatch errors as the learned policy drifts). Both avoid hand-specifying a reward function, but recovering the reward itself is often desirable for interpretability or to produce better policies than the demonstrator achieved.

## Maximum Entropy Inverse RL
Reward is fundamentally **non-identifiable** from demonstrations alone — infinitely many reward functions (even the all-zero reward) are compatible with any observed behavior. **Maximum Entropy IRL** (Ziebart et al., 2008) resolves this via the principle of maximum entropy: among all reward-induced trajectory distributions consistent with the data, prefer the one with highest entropy. This yields an exponential-family form, `P(τ) ∝ exp(R(τ))`, turning entropy-maximization into **maximum-likelihood estimation** of the reward parameters. The resulting gradient has an intuitive feature-matching form: the expert's observed feature expectations minus the feature expectations induced by the current reward's optimal policy. Computing the latter requires dynamic programming over a **known transition model** (solve for the optimal policy, then compute its state-visitation frequencies), so the algorithm alternates between policy optimization, visitation-frequency computation, and reward-gradient updates. Motivated originally by modeling taxi-driver route choices; later generalized (Finn et al., 2016) to deep/nonlinear rewards and to settings without a known dynamics model (closely related to GAIL).

## The Spectrum of Human-in-the-Loop Feedback
Frames demonstration-only imitation learning and DAGGER's constant coaching as two ends of a spectrum of how much effort a human is willing to give an RL agent (illustrated by MIT's "Sophie's Kitchen" robot-cooking assistant and the TAMER Tetris agent, which explicitly learned a reward model from live human feedback). **Pairwise preference comparisons** sit in a practical middle ground — easier for people to provide than a precise scalar reward, yet far less burdensome than constant coaching.

## Modeling Preferences: Bradley-Terry
The **Bradley-Terry model** assumes a latent reward underlies each comparison: `P(prefer i over j) = exp(R_i) / (exp(R_i) + exp(R_j))`. Distinguishes **Condorcet**, **Copeland**, and **Borda** notions of a "best" option among several when preferences come from pairwise comparisons — connecting RLHF to social-choice theory. Given labeled preference pairs, fitting the latent reward reduces to a **logistic-regression-style cross-entropy loss**, extendable to full trajectories by comparing summed per-step rewards between two trajectories.

## RLHF in Practice
The seminal "Deep RL from Human Preferences" (2017) trained a simulated robot to backflip using only ~900 pairwise video-clip comparisons — far more label-efficient than typical deep-RL training. The now-standard **ChatGPT-style RLHF pipeline**: (1) supervised fine-tuning on demonstrations (behavior cloning), (2) collect pairwise human comparisons of outputs and fit a reward model, (3) optimize the policy against that learned reward model using PPO. The reward model must generalize across effectively unlimited distinct prompts rather than a single task — framing RLHF as a form of **multi-task/meta reinforcement learning**. A simpler, RL-free alternative is **best-of-n sampling**: generate several completions and have the reward model pick the best, which performs surprisingly competitively with PPO.

## Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)
Motivated by the complexity and tuning fragility of the reward-model-then-PPO pipeline, **DPO** (Rafailov, Sharma, Mitchell et al.) exploits an exact one-to-one mapping between a language-model policy and a reward function to skip explicit reward modeling and RL entirely. The KL-regularized reward-maximization objective has a known closed-form optimal solution: a Boltzmann-style distribution reweighting the reference policy by the exponentiated reward, normalized by an intractable per-prompt partition function. Inverting this expresses the **reward as a function of the optimal policy** (a scaled log-ratio between the optimal and reference policy, plus a prompt-only term). Substituting this into the Bradley-Terry preference loss makes the partition-function term **cancel out** (it appears identically for both the preferred and dispreferred response), yielding the **DPO loss**: a cross-entropy loss directly on policy log-probability ratios of preferred vs. dispreferred responses relative to the reference policy.

On a controlled IMDB sentiment task (reward given by a known gold classifier), DPO traces a better **reward-vs-KL Pareto frontier** than tuned PPO — illustrating why comparing methods by a single win-rate number without reporting the KL budget is uninformative. PPO's weaker showing traced partly to high variance in reward estimates, mitigated simply by sampling more completions per prompt. DPO has since seen broad real-world adoption (most top models on a 2024 open LLM leaderboard, Mistral, and in part Llama 3).

**Extensions and open problems:** the DPO loss can be derived as an inverse-Q-learning algorithm under max-entropy RL, but does not transfer naively to classical control — control settings require reformulating preferences in terms of **regret** rather than reward. Non-transitive or population-conflicting preferences (e.g., rock-paper-scissors-style cycles) break single-reward-function modeling entirely (some preference sets are provably unsatisfiable by any reward function); **game-theoretic/Nash-based methods** (Nash learning from human feedback, direct Nash optimization) instead optimize expected win-rate against an adversary policy. **Reward hacking/overoptimization** is a finite-data phenomenon — the proxy reward keeps rising while the true reward falls, echoing DAgger-style distributional drift — and **weight-averaging/model-merging** of reward models or DPO checkpoints (echoing DQN's target-network Polyak averaging) empirically improves robustness. KL regularization is a crude uniform proxy for "stay conservative only where reward-model uncertainty is high"; **multi-objective DPO (MODPO)** extends the approach to multiple weighted objectives via a single preference-conditioned policy without retraining per tradeoff.

## Offline RL: Learning Better Policies from Fixed Data
Extends beyond preference-based RLHF to the general problem of learning (and evaluating) policies entirely from historical/logged data, without further environment interaction — a causal-inference-flavored "counterfactual RL" problem, motivated by cases (education, healthcare) where offline RL substantially outperformed imitation of the logged behavior policy (e.g., a ~30% persistence gain in an adaptive learning game).

**Model-based pitfall:** fitting a dynamics model and planning against it can look good under the model's own simulated evaluation while the *true* value of the resulting policy actually degrades — a symptom of model misspecification (the model can't represent the true dynamics even with infinite data), not mere overfitting. Lesson: evaluate policies with an independent estimator rather than trusting in-model performance; reweighting the model-fitting loss toward the target policy's likely state-action distribution (rather than the logging policy's) can help.

**Fitted Q Evaluation (FQE):** a DQN-like but `max`-free method that fits Q for one fixed target policy from logged (s, a, r, s') tuples; has finite-sample guarantees that scale with data size, desired accuracy, and a **concentrability coefficient** measuring the mismatch between the logged and target state-action distributions — but still assumes the Markov property and a realizable Q-function class.

**Importance sampling (IS):** a Markov-assumption-free alternative. Reweighting behavior-policy samples by the ratio of trajectory probabilities under the target vs. behavior policy gives an **unbiased** value estimate (Precup, Sutton & Singh, 2000); per-step transition probabilities cancel in the ratio, so no dynamics model is needed. Requires **coverage** — the behavior policy must assign nonzero probability to everything the target policy might do (otherwise even a lower bound is only available when rewards are all non-negative). Variance can explode over long horizons; mitigations include **per-decision importance sampling** (exploiting the policy-gradient-style causality argument that later actions can't affect earlier rewards) and weighted/doubly-robust estimators blending Markov and non-Markov assumptions.

**Pessimism under uncertainty:** classical off-policy optimization methods assumed coverage over *every* candidate policy, unrealistic for naturalistic (non-random) logging policies. A "chain MDP" example shows several prior conservative offline-RL methods behave non-monotonically with data size — intermediate data amounts get misled by noisy high-variance states into learning a worse policy than with either very little or lots of data. The fix: explicitly penalize insufficiently-visited state-actions (treat them as zero reward in Bellman backups), guaranteeing near-optimality only within the class of policies the data can actually support. Benchmarked favorably (MuJoCo/D4RL) against behavior cloning, BCQ, BEAR, uncertainty-penalized model-based planning, and Conservative Q-Learning (CQL). These ideas extend to **safety-constrained offline learning**, confidently certifying a new policy as better than the existing one before deployment (e.g., an FDA-approved diabetes insulin-dosing simulator).

# Companion Perspective: Stanford CS234 (Emma Brunskill), Lecture 14 — Monte Carlo Tree Search, AlphaGo & AlphaZero

**Overview:** A shift from the exploration unit into "reinforcement learning for computation" — using simulation-based planning to decide what to do *right now* in the current state rather than computing a global policy over the entire state space. Culminates in explaining how DeepMind's AlphaGo/AlphaZero combined these ideas with deep learning to master Go.

## Simulation-Based Search: Focus on the Current State
Core idea: rather than solving the whole MDP, concentrate all available computation on finding a good decision for the current state only — justified in domains with enormous state spaces (like Go, or policy problems like monetary policy) where computing a globally optimal policy everywhere is unnecessary and intractable. The simplest version: from the current state, consider each action, then roll out under a default policy to estimate Q(s,a) via Monte Carlo evaluation, and act greedily — essentially one step of policy improvement given a dynamics+reward model.

## Forward Search Trees and the Curse of Dimensionality
Generalizes this into an explicit lookahead tree (action nodes take a max over actions; state nodes take an expectation/average weighted by transition probabilities) — equivalent to repeated Bellman backups rooted at the current state. Naively this is intractable, since tree size scales as (state space × action space)^horizon. The key fix: expectations over next-states can be well-approximated by sampling rather than exhaustive enumeration, motivating **Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS)**.

## Upper Confidence Trees (UCT)
Combines tree search with bandit-style UCB: each action at each tree node maintains an empirical average return (from past rollouts through it) plus a shrinking UCB-style bonus term, and the next action to expand is chosen by maximizing average-return-plus-bonus. This selectively grows an unbalanced tree toward promising branches rather than expanding uniformly, letting sampling defeat the dimensionality curse — though it still requires trying every action at least once, problematic for very large action spaces. UCT-style methods are also naturally parallelizable.

## From UCT to AlphaGo/AlphaZero
Go is framed as a deterministic, zero-sum, two-player adversarial game (so tree backups use minimax rather than expectation) with sparse terminal-only rewards (making credit assignment hard) and an astronomically large state space. AlphaGo/AlphaZero build on UCT with several key innovations:
- **Self-play:** both players share the same evolving policy, giving a free, perfectly-matched opponent (bottlenecked only by compute) and crucially high reward density — matched opponents produce frequent wins/losses that provide useful learning signal, unlike playing a vastly stronger/weaker opponent (near-saturated or near-zero reward density, both uninformative).
- **Combined policy+value neural network:** a single deep network (e.g., a dual-headed ResNet) maps a board state (including short history, since Go rules depend on move repetition) to both a value estimate V(s) and a prior action-probability distribution P(s,·), replacing hand-crafted heuristics.
- **Modified UCT formula:** the exploration bonus incorporates the learned prior P(s,a) (to bias expansion toward promising moves in huge action spaces) and decays faster than standard UCT (favoring exploitation more aggressively, since the prior already screens out weak moves).
- **Leaf evaluation via the value network:** instead of rolling out to a terminal state, leaf nodes are evaluated directly via V(s), allowing truncation (e.g., after many moves) without losing an estimate of win probability.
- **Root action selection:** after many simulations (e.g., ~160,000 per move), the actual move is sampled from a distribution proportional to visit counts at the root (tempered by a temperature parameter), rather than simply taking the arg-max.
- **Training loop:** after each self-play game, the actual outcome (win/loss) and the MCTS-derived visit-count policies at each visited state become supervised-learning targets to retrain the combined policy/value network — repeated over enormous compute (tens of days across many TPUs).

## Empirical Findings
- **Architecture matters enormously:** switching from separate convolutional policy/value networks to a combined dual-headed ResNet produced large performance gains on identical training data.
- **MCTS at evaluation time is essential:** using the raw trained network without Monte Carlo tree search at decision time performs far worse than combining it with MCTS — the "last mile" of local search computation adds substantial value even after extensive training.
- **Emergent, non-human strategies:** AlphaZero required no human game data at all (only the rules), yet reached superhuman strength while discovering strategies that diverge from human play — later studied by researchers trying to teach these novel strategies back to human grandmasters.
- Briefly notes extensions of these computation-as-RL ideas beyond games (e.g., AlphaTensor for algorithm discovery) and that imperfect-information games (e.g., poker) require different methods (e.g., counterfactual regret minimization), since the deterministic-game-tree assumptions here don't hold.

# Companion Perspective: Stanford CS234 (Emma Brunskill & Guest Dan Weber), Lecture 15 — Value Alignment

**Overview:** Opens with follow-up clarifications on AlphaZero's UCT formula and root action-selection, then transitions into the course's final unit via a guest lecture by philosopher Dan Weber (Stanford HAI/Ethics in Society), examining the conceptual foundations of **value alignment** — i.e., what it actually means for an agent to "do what we want" once rewards come from human judgment rather than a fixed environment signal.

## AlphaZero Follow-Up
Clarifies that the UCT-style bonus term operates per tree **node** (not per raw state), so identical board positions reached via different paths maintain independent statistics; walks through how the root-level action-selection temperature parameter τ interpolates between a visit-count-proportional distribution (τ=1) and winner-take-all argmax (τ→0); reaffirms AlphaZero uses one shared network with two output heads (not separate policy/value networks), and that adding MCTS at test time atop a fully-trained network still produces dramatic performance gains. Notes successor work (MuZero, which drops the requirement of known game rules) and that imperfect-information games (e.g., poker) need different techniques since Go's full-observability/two-player assumptions don't hold.

## Framing Value Alignment: The Paperclip AI
Uses the classic "paperclip maximizer" thought experiment (Bostrom) — an agent that single-mindedly optimizes a literal objective and causes catastrophic harm — to motivate why naively optimizing a stated goal can diverge badly from what is actually wanted.

## Three Competing Notions of "What the User Really Wants"
1. **Intentions** — what the user meant. Technically hard (may require near-complete modeling of language/culture/context to disambiguate) and philosophically unstable (intentions can be based on incomplete information or flawed means-end reasoning, diverging from what the person truly wants).
2. **Preferences** — what the user actually prefers, inferred via revealed-preference methods (IRL, RLHF). Technically underdetermined (finite behavioral data can't uniquely pin down a preference function, especially for rare/emergency situations) and philosophically limited (preferences can themselves diverge from genuine well-being, e.g., a preference to smoke despite its harm).
3. **Objective best interest** — what's truly good for the person, independent of stated preference. Framed as fundamentally a philosophical rather than empirical question; surveys competing theories of well-being (hedonism/happiness, desire-satisfaction, and objective-list goods like health/knowledge/relationships), noting substantial practical overlap despite theoretical disagreement. Raises the central tension of **paternalism**: overriding stated preferences in the name of "best interest" risks disrespecting autonomy, itself a recognized good.

**Applied exercise (personalized news chatbot):** contrasts aligning to *preferences* (straightforward — optimize for what a user engages with) versus *best interest* (harder — risks paternalism, but may better guard against echo chambers by prioritizing accuracy and viewpoint diversity over merely preferred content); concludes these goals aren't mutually exclusive — some best-interest properties (e.g., factual accuracy, source diversity) may be safe to apply universally, while more subjective aspects are better left to individual preference.

## A Fourth Notion: Moral Rightness
Observes that alignment may need to extend beyond the *user's* interests to everyone's — e.g., paperclip AI's deeper failure is harming workers and the world, not just failing the user. This motivates appeal to **moral theory**, surveying: **consequentialism/utilitarianism** (maximize total good), **prioritarianism** (weight the worse-off more heavily, e.g., via log-weighting), **maximin/minimax** (optimize for the single worst-off individual), **satisficing** variants of any of the above, and **deontological** views (certain acts/rules — don't lie, steal, murder — hold as constraints regardless of consequences). Notes that moral theories are themselves deeply contested, and that even a demonstrably "correct" theory might be undesirable to hard-code if it conflicts with user values (paternalism and trust concerns again).

**Pragmatic alternative — common-sense/consensus morality:** rather than committing to one theory, align AI to ordinary shared human moral intuitions. Illustrated via a stark consequentialist counterexample — a literal-utilitarian "surgeon AI" might conclude that harvesting one bystander's organs to save five dying patients maximizes net good, a conclusion virtually no one accepts — showing how strict theoretical fidelity can produce dangerous edge-case behavior. Common-sense alignment trades this risk for human-like unpredictability in genuinely hard edge cases (e.g., trolley-problem-style dilemmas), closing on an open question: how acceptable is it for AI to be as morally uncertain in hard cases as humans themselves are?

# Companion Perspective: Stanford CS234 (Emma Brunskill), Lecture 16 — Course Review, Quiz Debrief & Closing Themes

This final lecture is primarily a course wrap-up rather than new technical content, so it's kept as its own standalone section instead of merging into the Lecture 15 alignment material.

**Quiz debrief highlights:**
- **PPO:** the first gradient step after a data-collection round is on-policy; any further gradient steps on that same batch are off-policy. No monotonic-improvement guarantee exists in general. Importance sampling corrects only for the action distribution, not the state-visitation mismatch — PPO instead relies on keeping the new policy "close enough" that visited-state drift stays small.
- **Value-alignment tie-in:** reaffirms that respecting user autonomy can justify supporting a user's "suboptimal" choice (the paternalism trade-off from the guest lecture), and that moral theories broaden the lens from individual utility to societal welfare.
- **MCTS/AlphaZero:** MCTS works whether or not the dynamics are Markov (it just needs to be able to sample a next state from whatever history it conditions on). Upper-confidence-bound methods remain useful even when the reward model is *known* (as in AlphaZero), since they still help prioritize which actions/branches to expand. AlphaZero specifically pairs MCTS with self-play to train a joint policy/value network.
- **PAC and regret theory:** a PAC algorithm guarantees an ε-optimal policy after a polynomial (in problem size and 1/ε) number of mistakes — not full optimality, and not necessarily sublinear regret, since making ε-sized mistakes indefinitely still yields linear regret. Minimizing regret and maximizing cumulative expected reward are equivalent framings of the same objective.

**Course retrospective — the three opening case studies revisited with full RL vocabulary:**
1. **AlphaTensor (discovering matrix-multiplication algorithms):** a multi-step RL problem combining MCTS with a shared policy/value network (à la AlphaZero). State = the sequence of tensor operations performed so far; reward = algorithm length, conditioned on only ever searching within a space of *provably correct* algorithms (so there's no distribution-shift risk at deployment — only risk of settling for a non-optimal-length algorithm). Unlike AlphaZero, search is discarded after training; the discovered algorithm alone is deployed at runtime.
2. **Plasma control for fusion:** model-based RL using a high-but-imperfect-fidelity physics simulator in place of real-world (unsafe) exploration, trained with an actor-critic method. Notably asymmetric architecture: the critic can be arbitrarily large/complex since it's only used offline during training, while the actor/control-policy must be small and fast enough to run in real time. Safety/simulator-trust concerns are handled by adding penalty terms to the reward for approaching regions considered unsafe or poorly modeled, rather than via hard constraints.
3. **COVID-19 border testing:** despite superficially resembling a sequential RL problem, this is actually a repeated, resource-constrained (budgeted) **batch bandit with delayed outcomes** (test results arrive ~24 hours later), which favors delay-robust algorithms like Thompson sampling. Complicated by multiple simultaneous constraints (test-site capacity, potential fairness considerations) and by the classic tension between easily-observed short-term proxy rewards and the true, slower-to-observe downstream outcome of interest (a tension also seen in advertising and streaming recommendation systems).

**Closing themes on RL's core nature and open challenges:**
- The defining difference from supervised/unsupervised learning: actions shape the future data distribution (states visited, not just rewards observed), creating an unavoidable distribution-shift/extrapolation risk whenever function approximation and off-policy or offline learning are combined. Different methods mitigate this differently: PPO uses gradient-step clipping, DAgger collects more expert labels where the learned policy diverges, and offline methods like CQL/MOPO/pessimistic Q-learning build in explicit pessimism.
- **Models vs. values vs. policies:** models reduce uncertainty estimation to a pure prediction problem (what state/reward comes next), which is easier to quantify using standard supervised-learning/statistics tools, whereas policy or Q-function uncertainty conflates "uncertainty about the world" with "uncertainty about what's best given that world." Q-functions summarize policy quality compactly (enabling greedy action selection via argmax); policies are the ultimate object of interest for actually making decisions.
- **Computation vs. data efficiency:** often interchangeable when a simulator is available (more compute per step ≈ more simulated samples), but genuinely distinct when data is intrinsically scarce (e.g., limited patients/students) or when real-time deployment constraints (self-driving cars, plasma control) force fast, low-compute actors regardless of how much offline compute was used to train them.
- **Open challenges flagged for the field:** the need for more robust, automatic hyperparameter/model-selection methods (since real deployments often can't be tuned the way homework can); explicit mechanisms letting practitioners choose a point on the data-vs-computation efficiency frontier; the hybrid offline/online ("experimental design") regime as an underexplored middle ground; questioning whether the standard MDP formulation is always the right one to solve, even when it technically applies; the relatively unexplored potential of multi-task/transfer learning in RL (as opposed to learning from scratch per task); richer, non-scalar feedback (preferences, natural-language feedback) enabled by LLMs; non-stochastic, multi-agent settings (cooperative or adversarial); and the trade-off between fully integrated learning-and-planning versus modular system-identification-then-planning approaches. The lecture closes by emphasizing the field's large remaining room for real-world decision-making impact.
